## Slide 03 — What Is an Atom? From Atomos to Dalton's Theory (1808)
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 01

**On-slide speaking points:**
- The word atom comes from Greek atomos, meaning indivisible — first a philosophical idea, not an experiment.
- In 1808 John Dalton turned philosophy into testable chemistry: matter is made of tiny particles called atoms.
- Dalton's rules: atoms of one element are identical, and atoms combine in fixed ratios to form compounds.
- Example the class can weigh: carbon and oxygen always combine in fixed mass ratios, never arbitrary amounts.
- In Dalton's picture the atom was a tiny solid ball that could never be broken — a claim later experiments destroyed.

## Slide 04 — Dalton's Solid Ball — What It Explained and Why It Was Accepted
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 01

**On-slide speaking points:**
- The solid ball explained why elements combine in fixed, predictable ratios instead of melting together arbitrarily.
- It gave chemistry measurable atoms: each element had a characteristic combining mass chemists could weigh in the lab.
- With no evidence of inner parts for nearly a century, an indivisible sphere was the simplest picture that worked.
- Acceptance came from usefulness: the model predicted combining behaviour correctly even though its hardness was assumed.
- Its weakness was structural silence: a solid ball cannot emit electrons, conduct in discharge tubes, or give line spectra.

## Slide 06 — The Three Subatomic Particles — Electron, Proton, Neutron
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 01

**On-slide speaking points:**
- The electron carries −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C with a tiny mass of ~9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg — the lightest part, first proof atoms break open.
- The proton carries the same charge but positive (+1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) and is ~1836 times heavier than the electron.
- The neutron, found later by Chadwick in 1932, has no charge and a mass almost equal to the proton.
- Neutrality rule: total positive charge equals total negative charge, so the whole atom is electrically neutral.

## Slide 09 — Negative Charge Proved — Bending in Electric and Magnetic Fields
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 02

**On-slide speaking points:**
- In an electric field the rays bend toward the positively charged plate — only a negative charge is pulled that way.
- Magnetic fields deflect them likewise, confirming the beam responds as moving charge, not as light waves.
- The undeflected straight path versus the bent path is the whole argument: the difference is caused by charge.
- Light would ignore both fields; these rays obey both — so they are streams of charged particles.
- Charge is now settled; the next measurement asks how light each particle is — the famous ratio.

## Slide 10 — How Light Is the Electron? e/m = 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 02

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Thomson balanced electric against magnetic deflection until the two bends cancelled — then computed e/m = 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg.
- Here e is the particle's charge in coulombs and m its mass in kilograms, so the ratio reads coulombs per kilogram.
- The value is ~1800 times larger than for a hydrogen ion — for equal charge the mass must be ~1800 times smaller.
- The same ratio appeared with every gas and every cathode metal — so electrons are a common part of every atom.
- Later Millikan's oil-drop experiment fixed the charge e separately; combined with e/m this gives the electron mass. The atom is divisible.

## Slide 14 — Failure One — Large-Angle Scattering Is Impossible in Pudding
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 04

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Spread-out positive charge makes only a weak electric field inside the atom, so a passing alpha feels a slight push.
- The model therefore predicts every alpha particle suffers only a tiny deflection — a small wiggle, never a rebound.
- Worse, electrons cannot rescue the prediction: an alpha is ~7300 times heavier than an electron.
- Source analogy: a bus hitting ping-pong balls — the bus never bounces back, no matter how many balls it meets.
- Experiments showed violent large-angle rebounds instead — the first direct clash between pudding and reality.

## Slide 18 — The Probe — Alpha Particles in a Lead Collimator
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 05

**On-slide speaking points:**

- Alpha particles are helium nuclei — two protons plus two neutrons — carrying charge +2e.
- They leave the source at ~10⁷ metres per second: heavy and fast, ideal hammers for testing atomic structure.

## Slide 23 — Impact Parameter — Why the Same Beam Gives Three Fates
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 06

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Definition: the impact parameter b is the perpendicular distance between the alpha's original path and the nucleus centre.
- In plain words, b measures how off-centre the aim is — a wide miss has large b, a near hit has small b.
- Large b: the alpha passes far away, feels only weak distant repulsion, and sails on nearly straight — the common fate.
- Small b: the alpha dives through the strong near-nucleus field and swings through a large angle — the rare 0.14% tail.
- Diagram: trace both paths from the same incoming direction; the nucleus sits at the bend centre of each.

## Slide 31 — Why Classical Orbits Collapse — The ~10⁻¹¹ s Death Sentence
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 09

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Circular motion constantly changes direction, and changing direction is acceleration — orbiting means accelerating.
- Classical electrodynamics (Maxwell) says an accelerated charge radiates electromagnetic waves and loses energy.
- Losing energy shrinks the orbit, which speeds the spiral: the electron should crash into the nucleus.
- The source's timescale is ~10⁻¹¹ seconds — clearly labelled a CLASSICAL PREDICTION, never a measurement.
- Reality check: real atoms are stable indefinitely — so the prediction refutes the classical orbit, not the atom.

## Slide 33 — Prediction vs Measurement — How Physics Judges a Theory
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 09

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Classical PREDICTIONS: atomic collapse in ~10⁻¹¹ s and a continuous spectrum — what the old theory demands.
- Experimental MEASUREMENTS: atoms are stable and spectra are sharp lines — what nature actually shows.
- Physics keeps measurements and discards the theory: classical laws fail at the atomic scale, however grand elsewhere.
- This is not a draw — two independent failures (stability, spectra) convict the classical orbit twice over.

## Slide 34 — Bohr's Fix, Idea 1 (1913) — Stationary Orbits That Do Not Radiate
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 10

## Slide 35 — Bohr's Fix, Idea 2 — Only Whole-Number Orbits Are Allowed
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 10

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Allowed orbits satisfy L = n·h/2π, with n = 1, 2, 3, and so on — no fractions, no in-betweens.
- Term by term: L is the electron's angular momentum, n the orbit number, h Planck's constant [SUPPL: h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s].
- Physically this says circling motion comes in whole steps — orbits are quantised like rungs, not a ramp.
- Forbidden zones follow: an electron can sit on rung 1 or 2, but never hover halfway between them.
- With silent rungs fixed, one rule remains: how does light ever get in or out? Idea 3 answers.

## Slide 36 — Bohr's Fix, Idea 3 — Light Only in Quantum Jumps
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 10

**On-slide speaking points:**

- Fixed gaps give fixed photon energies, hence fixed spectral lines — hydrogen's spectrum is explained at last.

## Slide 38 — Beyond Bohr — Probability Clouds Replace Fixed Paths
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 10

**On-slide speaking points:**
- Heavier atoms overflow Bohr's single-electron arithmetic, so physics rebuilt the foundation as wave mechanics.
- Heisenberg's uncertainty principle [SUPPL, 1927]: an electron's exact path and speed cannot be known together.
- Schrödinger's wave equation [SUPPL, 1926] replaces paths with wavefunctions, where |ψ|² is the probability per region.

- Exam trap, say it twice: a Bohr ORBIT is a fixed ring, a quantum ORBITAL is a probable cloud — never swap the words.
- Orbitals carry quantum numbers n, l, m, s — the address system behind the periodic table you already use.
