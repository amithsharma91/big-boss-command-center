## Slide 03 — What Is an Atom? From 'Atomos' to Dalton's Scientific Theory (1808)
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 01

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- The word atom comes from Greek atomos, meaning indivisible — an old philosophical idea that Dalton turned into testable chemistry in 1808.
▸  Dalton proposed: matter is made of tiny atoms; atoms of one element are identical; atoms combine in fixed ratios to form compounds — pictured as a tiny solid ball that can never be broken.
▸  Dalton's picture explained combining ratios chemists could weigh, so it survived nearly a century despite having no internal structure.
▸  Later, electrolysis and electric discharge through gases showed atoms are connected to electricity — they must contain smaller charged parts, so atoms are divisible.
▸  That forces our central question, which atomic models try to answer: how are the inner charged particles arranged inside the atom?

## Slide 04 — The Three Subatomic Particles — Electron, Proton, Neutron
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 01

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- The electron carries −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C with a tiny mass of ~9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg — the lightest particle, first proof that atoms can be broken apart.
▸  The proton carries the same charge but positive (+1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) and is ~1836 times heavier than the electron — it anchors the atom's positive charge.
▸  The neutron, found later by Chadwick in 1932, has no charge and a mass almost equal to the proton — it completes the trio but post-dates Thomson and Rutherford.
▸  Together they explain neutrality: total positive charge equals total negative charge, so the whole atom is electrically neutral overall.
▸  TABLE (right): read charge, mass and discoverer row by row — we spend the rest of the talk arranging exactly these parts.

## Slide 06 — Properties of Cathode Rays — Why They Must Be Negative Particles
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 02

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Cathode rays travel in straight lines: they cast a sharp shadow of any object placed in their path — light-like straightness, but they are not light.
▸  They carry energy and momentum: they spin a tiny paddle wheel placed in the beam and make glass or a fluorescent screen glow where they hit.
▸  Crucial test — in an electric field they bend toward the positive plate, proving they carry negative charge; magnetic fields deflect them likewise.
▸  Thomson balanced electric against magnetic deflection until they cancelled, then computed e/m = 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg — ~1800× larger than a hydrogen ion, so the particle is extremely light.
▸  The same e/m appeared whatever gas or cathode metal he used — so electrons are a common part of every atom; later Millikan's oil-drop fixed the charge e separately. Conclusion: the atom is divisible.

**Visual — what to point at:** custom deflection schematic: red + plate top, navy - plate bottom, cyan beam bending upward + embedded modern CRT photo (Hebels, PD, illustrative).

## Slide 08 — Why Thomson's Model Failed — Four Problems Before a Single Gold-Foil Shot
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 04

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Large-angle scattering impossible: spread-out positive charge gives only a weak internal field, predicting tiny deflections — yet experiments later showed violent rebounds.
▸  Electrons cannot rescue it: an alpha particle is ~7300× heavier than an electron — like a bus hitting ping-pong balls, the bus will not bounce back.
▸  No nucleus, no concentration: the model assumes positive charge fills the atom, leaving no dense centre that could throw back a fast heavy particle.
▸  Wrong light and fragile stability: vibrating electrons could not give sharp line spectra at exact wavelengths, and static charges cannot balance stably by electrostatics alone.
▸  Verdict: the model explained neutrality and removable electrons, but failed scattering, nucleus, line spectra and stability — so Rutherford tested it directly.

**Visual — what to point at:** red-bordered card: 'only tiny nudges' prediction, diffuse atom with straight cyan pass + bus-vs-ping-pong mass analogy (7300x).

## Slide 09 — Rutherford's Gold-Foil Experiment (1909, Manchester) — Setup That Broke the Pudding
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 05

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Aim (Rutherford, Geiger & Marsden, ~1909, Manchester): fire fast heavy particles at atoms and watch deflections — a direct test of Thomson's spread-charge prediction.
▸  Alpha source: radioactive radium/polonium emitting helium nuclei — charge +2e, speed ~10⁷ m/s — housed in a lead block with a narrow hole that makes a thin beam (lead absorbs the rest).
▸  Target: very thin gold foil, only a few hundred atoms thick — gold chosen because it hammers extremely thin and its heavy nucleus (Z = +79) gives strong repulsion.
▸  Detector: zinc-sulphide screen around the foil flashes (scintillation) wherever an alpha hits; a microscope counts flashes angle by angle — whole apparatus in vacuum.
▸  Thomson predicted: almost all alphas through with only small deflections. What happened surprised everyone — next slide. [Historic Geiger–Marsden diagram inset.]

## Slide 10 — Three Observations — What Happened vs What It Meant (Keep Them Separate)
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 06

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- OBS. 1 — Most alphas passed straight through with zero deflection. CONCLUSION: the atom is mostly empty space — the beam usually meets nothing.
▸  OBS. 2 — Some alphas deflected by small angles (~0.14% by more than 1°). CONCLUSION: concentrated positive charge exists inside and repels the +2e alphas.
▸  OBS. 3 — Very few, ~1 in 8000, scattered by >90°, some straight back at 180°. CONCLUSION: positive charge + almost all mass sit in a tiny dense nucleus — only a head-on hit can reverse a fast heavy alpha.
▸  Rutherford's image: like firing a huge shell at tissue paper and watching it come back — the most surprising result, impossible for spread charge.
▸  Discipline: left column is what was SEEN (counts, angles); right column is what was INFERRED — never mix them when the teacher probes.

## Slide 11 — Impact Parameter — Why Aim Decides Deflection
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 06

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Definition: impact parameter b = perpendicular distance between the alpha's original straight path and the nucleus centre — how 'off-centre' the aim is.
▸  Large b (wide miss): alpha feels only weak distant repulsion and sails on with a tiny deflection — the common case, hence mostly straight-through.
▸  Small b (close pass): alpha dives through the strong near-nucleus field and swings through a large angle — the rare ~0.14% tail.
▸  Zero b (head-on, b = 0): alpha stops, turns and bounces back at 180° — the ultra-rare ~1 in 8000 events that killed Thomson's model.
▸  Rule to memorize: smaller impact parameter → closer approach → larger deflection; draw the three paths with the nucleus as the bending centre.

**Visual — what to point at:** custom 3-path diagram around amber nucleus with b-bracket: large b tiny bend, small b large bend, b=0 180-degree back.

**Narration (speaker notes):** PRESENTER 06 — Impact Parameter — Why Aim Decides Deflection. Notes (Member 06): Trace large/small/zero paths on diagram. Emphasise b as aiming error. HANDOFF: 'Member 07 builds the new model.'

## Slide 12 — Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911) — Tiny Dense Nucleus + Orbiting Electrons
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 07

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Core claim (1911): a small, dense, positively charged nucleus sits at the centre holding most of the atom's mass; electrons revolve around it in circular orbits.
▸  Mostly empty space: the nucleus is ~10⁴–10⁵× smaller than the atom, so most alphas never approach one — straight-through explained at once.
▸  Also called the planetary model: electrons orbit like planets, with electrostatic attraction playing the role gravity plays for planets.
▸  Force picture (historical): Coulomb attraction provides centripetal force —  F(Coulomb) = m·v²/r  — nucleus pulls in, circular motion needs inward force.
▸  Honest label: this orbit picture is HISTORICAL — it explains scattering beautifully but keeps classical orbits that later fail on stability (Member 9).

## Slide 16 — Continuous vs Line Spectra — The Fingerprint Classical Physics Cannot Print
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 09

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Classical expectation: a spiralling electron's frequency changes continuously, so it should emit ALL wavelengths — a smooth rainbow continuum.
▸  Reality: hydrogen and other atoms emit only SHARP lines at specific wavelengths — e.g. hydrogen's visible Balmer series (red, cyan-blue, violet lines on the photo right).
▸  Those lines follow the Rydberg formula (supplementary: 1/λ = R(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), R ≈ 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹) — exact integer pattern Rutherford's spirals cannot give.
▸  So two independent failures: atoms do NOT collapse (stable) and do NOT glow continuously (line spectra) — classical physics fails at atomic scale.
▸  A new theory must quantise something — Member 10 shows Bohr's first fix. [Spectrum photo: Umop503, CC0.]

## Slide 17 — Bohr's Fix (1913) — Stationary Orbits, Quantised Angular Momentum, Quantum Jumps
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 10

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Postulate 1 — Stationary orbits: electrons may circle only in certain allowed orbits and radiate NO energy while in them — stability imposed by rule.
▸  Postulate 2 — Quantised angular momentum: only orbits with  L = n·h/2π,  n = 1, 2, 3… are allowed — orbits are quantised in whole steps, nothing between.
▸  Postulate 3 — Quantum jumps: light is emitted/absorbed ONLY when an electron jumps between orbits, carrying  ΔE = h·ν  — the energy difference, not a spiral glow.
▸  Payoff: jumps between fixed levels give fixed photon energies → sharp lines — hydrogen's spectrum explained for the first time.
▸  Portrait: Niels Bohr c.1922 (public domain, Nobel year) — his model works for hydrogen-like atoms; multi-electron atoms need the full quantum model next.

**Visual — what to point at:** 3 postulate blocks + Bohr c.1922 portrait (PD); central equations L = n h/2pi and DeltaE = h nu.

## Slide 19 — Modern Quantum Model — From Fixed Orbits to Probability Orbitals
**Presenter:** PRESENTER 10

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Heisenberg (1927, supplementary context): we cannot know an electron's exact path and speed together — exact classical trajectories are unknowable in principle.
▸  Schrödinger's wave equation (1926) replaces paths with wavefunctions: |ψ|² gives the PROBABILITY of finding the electron in each region of space.
▸  An orbital is that region — e.g. spherical 1s cloud densest at centre (image right: brighter = likelier; PoorLeno, public domain) — described by quantum numbers n, l, m, s.
▸  ORBIT vs ORBITAL (exam trap): Bohr orbit = fixed circular path with exact radius; quantum orbital = fuzzy probability cloud with no exact path — never swap the words.
▸  Result: stability and spectra emerge naturally from allowed wave patterns — the atom stays mostly empty, now with electrons as clouds, not planets.

**Visual — what to point at:** embedded H density plots (PoorLeno PD, brighter=likelier) + ORBIT vs ORBITAL contrast blocks; quantum numbers n,l,m,s.

## Slide 21 — Quick Revision — Eight Questions to Lock the Story
**Presenter:** PRESENTER — ALL MEMBERS

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Q1: Who found the electron, when? — Thomson, 1897, via cathode rays with e/m = 1.76×10¹¹ C/kg, same for all gases/metals.
▸  Q2: What IS Thomson's model? — Positive sphere ~10⁻¹⁰ m with embedded electrons, neutral, NO nucleus (pudding/watermelon).
▸  Q3: What was Rutherford's experiment? — 1909 gold-foil alpha scattering: +2e alphas at ~10⁷ m/s onto thin gold (Z=79), ZnS flashes in vacuum.
▸  Q4: Three results? — Most straight (empty space); ~0.14% small angles (positive charge); ~1 in 8000 >90°/back (tiny dense nucleus).

## Slide 24 — Conclusion — From Solid Ball to Probability Cloud
**Presenter:** MEMBERS 01–10

**On-slide speaking points (read aloud in your own words):**
- Dalton (1808) gave us atoms; Thomson (1897/1904) gave us the electron and the first inner model — neutral but nucleus-free and soon broken.
▸  Rutherford (1909–1911) gave us the nucleus and the planetary picture — tiny dense centre (10⁻¹⁵–10⁻¹⁴ m) in vast emptiness (10⁻¹⁰ m) — broken in turn by stability and spectra.
▸  Bohr (1913) quantised the orbits (L = n·h/2π, ΔE = hν, En = −13.6/n² eV) and saved hydrogen's lines; quantum theory (Heisenberg–Schrödinger) replaced orbits with orbitals.
▸  Method to remember: experiment breaks theory, numbers decide — e/m, 1 in 8000, 10⁻¹⁰ m, 10⁻¹¹ s, −13.6 eV are the fingerprints of each break.
